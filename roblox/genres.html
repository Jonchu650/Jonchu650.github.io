§ 14  ·  Playbook

Priorities by genre

The ladder is the same for everyone. Which rungs you are naturally good at is not. Every genre has a structural strength it can coast on and a structural weakness that quietly caps it.

How to use this page

Find your genre, identify your structural weakness, and fix that rather than deepening the strength you already have. Because the ranking signals are averages and several of them are measured in separate D1 / D2–7 / D8–28 windows, the signal you are worst at is almost always where the cheapest distribution is hiding.

02The genres in detail

Simulator and collect-em-up

The dominant commercial genre, and the one with the most crowded tiles. Its structural advantages are real: passive or offline progression gives you play days almost for free, and the collection fantasy naturally supports a price ladder, which feeds spend days.

The structural weakness is play through rate and first-minute bounce. Fifty games in this genre share a visual language, so the tile does not differentiate and the first thirty seconds look like every other one. The two highest-return moves are a thumbnail that shows the collection rather than the environment, and handing the player their first collectible immediately — before any challenge.

  • Cap offline accumulation at roughly a day, so returning tomorrow specifically is rewarded
  • Always display the next rarity tier, so there is a visible reason not to log off
  • Make bases or collections visitable — this converts a solo genre into co-play signal cheaply
  • Add one cheap daily-refreshing consumable. Spend days is listed above Robux spent for a reason

Obby and platformer

The clearest tile on the platform and the worst retention profile on it. Obby games convert impressions into plays better than almost anything else and then lose those players immediately — the genre sits last on D1, D7 and D30 in every dataset.

Because play through rate is already strong, effectively all of your available upside is in play days and playtime. Treat return-visit design as the entire product problem, not a feature.

  • A daily stage or daily challenge, so the game is different tomorrow
  • Persistent progression across courses — something that carries between sessions
  • Leaderboards and time trials, which convert a finite course into a repeatable one
  • Checkpoint generosity tuned by data, not instinct: instrument each stage as a funnel step

Horror

Horror over-performs on co-play — it is one of the few genres where playing with friends is straightforwardly better — and under-performs badly on D7 and D30, because knowledge destroys fear. A player who has seen the entity and learned the map has finished your game without noticing.

  • Procedural or semi-procedural layouts so knowledge decays more slowly
  • Multiple entities with different behaviours, revealed over time
  • Lean hard into invite prompts — this genre's co-play signal is its competitive advantage
  • Instrument which entity or map players quit after, as a custom field on a session-end event

RPG and progression-heavy games

The best retention profile in the data and the worst onboarding problem. RPGs front-load character creation, class choice, tutorials and systems explanation — all before anything fun happens. That is directly at odds with the second-highest-weighted ranking signal.

  • Put combat before character creation. Let them play, then choose
  • Defer systems explanation until the system is relevant
  • Instrument the onboarding funnel densely — RPGs have more steps to lose people at
  • Your D7 and D30 strength means you can afford to be aggressive about cutting the first session

PvP, fighting and battlegrounds

Strong on play days, qualified sessions and co-play; weak on new-player bounce, because a new player's first match is usually against someone who has played for months. The genre's retention problem is a matchmaking problem.

  • Protect new players for their first several matches — separate queue, bot fill, or handicap
  • Guarantee an early win. The first session must contain one
  • Watch the D1 window specifically on play days and playtime; the drop-off is concentrated there
  • Check queue times by region — a long wait reads as a bounce

Social, roleplay and hangout

Excellent on co-play days and D30, and structurally weak on monetization frequency, because the only natural purchases are cosmetic and durable. That means spend days — a second-tier signal — tends to sit near zero.

  • Give players a loop, not just a space. Something to do together beats somewhere to be together
  • Cheap, frequently-rotating cosmetics generate spend days that a permanent pass cannot
  • Private servers are a natural fit and feed co-play signal directly
  • Watch session length carefully: idle time in a social space inflates it without adding value

03Niche and under-served genres

The obvious argument for a crowded genre is that the audience is proven. The counter-argument now has platform support behind it.

Non-uniqueness is an explicit penalty

Games whose metadata and place files closely resemble existing games are no longer prioritised for recommendations and rank lower in search. Cloning a trending title has moved from a shortcut to a cost.

Standout Games rewards the opposite

A hand-curated Home sort specifically for novel mechanics, distinctive visuals, or an under-represented genre. There is no equivalent sort for “well-executed clone”.

Semantic search closes the discovery gap

Search now understands natural-language queries across supported languages. A niche game with accurate, descriptive metadata is findable in a way it was not a few years ago.

The honest trade-off

A niche game has less competition and a smaller addressable audience, which means retrieval is harder but ranking is easier — a devoted small audience produces strong per-user averages, and per-user averages are exactly what the ranking stage reads. The practical risk is not ranking; it is getting into the candidate pool at all. That is the one problem paid traffic and social pushes genuinely solve.

Rather than trusting a genre label, derive it. Open the Home Recommendations dashboard and rank your eight signals against the similar-games benchmark.

The transferable version of this page

Genres are a shortcut for a question you can answer directly: which of the eight signals does my game's shape make easy, and which does it make hard? Write that down once, honestly, and it will tell you what to build for the next six months more reliably than any benchmark table.
