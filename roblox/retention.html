§ 04  ·  Metrics

Retention

The metric family Roblox says to fix before anything else, and the one its recommendation system is explicitly being steered toward. Each of D1, D7 and D30 fails for a different reason and is fixed by a different system.

01The three horizons

Roblox maps each retention window to a specific part of your game. This mapping is the most useful diagnostic shortcut in the whole dashboard: which number is low tells you which system is broken.

MetricDefinitionA low number meansFix
D1 retentionPercentage of new users who return after their first session endsThe first session was confusing, frustrating, or did not reach the funCore loop & first-time user experience
D7 retentionPercentage of new users who return on the 8th dayNo tangible reason to imagine still playing a week outProgression system
D30 retentionPercentage of new users who return on the 31st dayNothing to do after the main objectives are completeEndgame & social systems
Roblox's own framing. Note that all three are measured against the same cohort — users who first played on a given date — so a single bad acquisition day shows up as a simultaneous dip on all three charts.
Read the x-axis correctly

The x-axis on all three retention charts is the cohort's first play date, not the return date. The point at 06/20 on the D7 chart means “users who first played on 06/20 and came back a week later.” That data does not appear until 06/27. Your D7 and D30 charts will always have empty tails — that is the design, not a bug.

The practical benefit: a change you ship on a given day lines up vertically across all three charts, so you can see its full downstream effect on one date.

02What the numbers actually are

There are three different sets of retention numbers circulating, they disagree wildly, and the reason is sampling. Here is all three, honestly labelled.

Which number should you actually use

Your own dashboard's similar-games benchmark, and nothing else. It is computed daily against games enjoyed by players similar to yours, which is the only comparison that controls for genre, audience age and region at once.

Use the platform-wide percentiles for a reality check when someone tells you a 30% D1 is ‘normal’. Across the whole platform, the 99th percentile is around 22%. Numbers above that come from samples of already-successful, already-promoted games. Holding yourself to them before launch is a good way to conclude a healthy game is failing.

03Fixing D1: the core loop and the first session

Core loop

Fix first

The actions players repeat to make progress within a single session.

Examples
Adopt → train → level up a pet. Manage → balance → compete in a tycoon
Step 1
Identify the core steps and make each one clear and fun on its own
Step 2
Balance the challenge — avoid both frustration and boredom
Step 3
Reward loop completion with feedback, currency, items and achievements

First-time user experience

  • Brief tutorial or contextual tooltips to guide players through the core loop. Roblox is explicit: avoid long tutorials, get to the fun fast.
  • A joyful moment the first time a player completes the loop — the payoff has to land, not just resolve.
  • Preview the progress available if they complete the loop repeatedly. Show the ladder, not just the first rung.
  • Under five minutes from join to fun. Roblox states this as a target, not an aspiration.

Instrument it, then fix what the data says

Roblox's recommended method, which is also the cheapest analytics work you will ever do:

  1. List the steps of your core loop. For a pet game: adopt a pet, train a pet, level up a pet.
  2. Track the completion rate of each step as an onboarding funnel. Track negative outcomes too — if there is an NPC battle, log both wins and losses.
  3. Find the biggest drop-off and reduce its friction. In Roblox's own worked example from the Plant reference project, the funnel showed a 70% drop between step 1 (“In Farm”) and step 2 (“Plant Seed”). The fix was contextual prompts above the flowerpots.

See Instrumentation for the code. An onboarding funnel takes about twenty minutes to add and is the highest-value analytics work available to a pre-launch game.

04Fixing D7: the progression system

D7 is a question about whether a player can picture themselves still playing next week. That requires a visible ladder with rungs they have not reached.

Set goals at two ranges

Short-term goals give instant gratification and feedback. Long-term goals give direction over weeks. A game with only one kind stalls.

Add content variety

New modes, inventory items, social features. Variety is what prevents a good loop from becoming a boring one.

Balance the difficulty

Too fast and they exhaust your content. Too slow and they quit. This is the single most common cause of a weak D7.

How to measure it: track the number of players at each progression level by granting a special in-game item or firing a custom event on level-up. The distribution across levels tells you where players stall and where they sprint. If most of your week-one players are clustered at level 2, your level 2→3 gate is too expensive.

05Fixing D30: the endgame

D30 asks what exists after the main objectives are done. Roblox names two answers.

More content, on a cadence

The published rhythm: smaller updates to existing mechanics every 2–4 weeks, and bigger feature updates every 2–3 months. This is the only concrete update-frequency guidance Roblox gives, and it is worth treating as a schedule rather than a suggestion — a predictable cadence is also what makes experience events and notifications work.

Social mechanics

Trading, parties, guilds, PvP, tournaments and leaderboards keep players engaged with each other while they wait for you to build. They also feed the intentional co-play days ranking signal, which makes them one of the few investments that pays into two tiers at once.

06Cohort analysis

The cohort table at the bottom of the retention dashboard is the most under-used view on the Creator Hub. It shows daily cohorts across their first 10 days, or weekly cohorts across 10 weeks.

Down-funnel metricWhat it answers
7D playtime per user (cumulative)Did this cohort engage more than previous ones
7D player conversion rate (cumulative)Did this cohort start spending faster
7D revenue per user (cumulative)Early monetization quality of the cohort
30D revenue per user (cumulative)Full monetization value of the cohort
Use these to evaluate campaigns and events properly: did the players an event brought in actually stick and spend, or did they inflate DAU for a weekend and leave?
The most useful thing cohorts tell you

A dip across all three retention charts on the same date usually means a large influx of new users that day — Roblox calls this out explicitly as a typical and temporary outcome. Your retention did not get worse; your mix got more casual. Check whether new users spiked before you start rewriting your tutorial.

07The retention checklist

  • An onboarding funnel is instrumented and you know your largest drop-off step by name
  • A new player reaches a genuine payoff in under five minutes
  • There is something to lose by not logging in tomorrow
  • A week-one player can name a goal they are working toward
  • A month-one player has social systems or competition to fall back on between updates
  • You ship a small update every 2–4 weeks and a large one every 2–3 months
  • You check retention by cohort after every update, segmented by When user first played
